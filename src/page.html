The problem

Why there is no formula

Newton solved two bodies completely in 1687: each follows a conic section, and you can read its position at any time off a closed expression. The two-body problem has enough conserved quantities (energy, momentum, angular momentum, and the orbit's orientation) to pin the motion down.

Three bodies have 18 degrees of freedom in 3D and only 10 classical conserved quantities. In 1887 Bruns showed there are no other algebraic integrals, and Poincaré (1890) extended that to analytic ones. Without more integrals, the motion can't be reduced to quadratures. While proving this, Poincaré found trajectories that tangle so densely that tiny changes in the start send them anywhere. That observation is the origin of chaos theory.

A solution does exist. Karl Sundman proved in 1912 that, unless all three collide at once, the motion is given by a convergent power series. It is useless in practice: getting astronomical accuracy would take on the order of 108,000,000 terms.

So "unsolvable" means no closed form and no long-range shortcut. The trajectory is fully determined, and a computer can follow it as precisely as you can afford. In a chaotic case each extra digit of starting precision buys roughly the same fixed amount of extra forecast time. Drag the shadow-offset slider from 10⁻³ to 10⁻¹² on the Pythagorean case and watch the horizon move only a little.

That leaves three kinds of answer, all in the simulator: exact special solutions, periodic orbits found by search, and statistical predictions of how a chaotic encounter ends.

What "solved" has meant

Three centuries of partial answers

  1. 1767Euler: three bodies on a rotating straight line. Exact, but unstable.
  2. 1772Lagrange: the rotating equilateral triangle, for any masses. Stable only when one mass dominates. Jupiter's Trojan asteroids sit in it.
  3. 1887–90Bruns, Poincaré: no further algebraic (Bruns) or analytic (Poincaré) integrals exist, so there is no general closed-form solution. Chaos is discovered.
  4. 1912Sundman: a convergent series solution exists. Far too slow to use.
  5. 1913Burrau poses the Pythagorean problem. In 1967 Szebehely and Peters compute it to the end: the lightest body is ejected.
  6. 1993Moore finds the figure-eight numerically. Chenciner and Montgomery prove it exists in 2000.
  7. 2013Šuvakov and Dmitrašinović find 13 new distinct periodic orbits (butterfly, moth, yin-yang…) by computer search and sort them into classes by topology.
  8. 2017+Li and Liao report 695 families on a supercomputer. A 2023 search by Hristov and colleagues finds 12,409 distinct equal-mass free-fall orbits.
  9. 2019Stone and Leigh (Nature) give a statistical solution: for a chaotic encounter, the probability of each outcome, not the path. Ginat and Perets (2021) do the same with a random-walk model.
  10. 2026Claims of a general exact solution keep appearing as unreviewed preprints, including one on Zenodo in March 2026. None has been accepted, and a closed form would contradict Bruns and Poincaré unless it redefines the question.
The statistical solution

Who gets thrown out?

You can't predict one chaotic encounter, but you can predict many. Start three bodies at random in a bound cluster and run until one escapes for good. The statistical theories predict that the lightest body is ejected most often, leaving the heavier two as a binary. Run it yourself.

Runs in a background thread. 300 encounters take roughly 10–20 seconds.
Fiction check

The novel's three suns

Liu Cixin's The Three-Body Problem puts its aliens on a planet with three suns. The book describes them only as the inhabitants experience them: stable eras when the planet circles one sun, chaotic eras of scorching heat or deep cold, and "flying stars", suns so distant they look like stars. Two flying stars mean a stable era is coming; three mean bitter cold. There are no masses, distances or periods, so the orbits can't be reconstructed. They can be tested for consistency, though.

The eras are good physics. A planet orbiting one sun while the other two are far away is exactly the condition this simulator uses to call an era stable. Load Three suns, one planet to watch eras come and go.

How long it lasts is not. In 400 random systems like that one, the planet was thrown out or fell into a sun after a median of 13 years. Most lost planets had one stable era or none; only 13 of 313 had three or more. The novel has civilization after civilization. Planets that did last mostly did so because the suns stopped being chaotic. And the real α Centauri the book names is the opposite case: a tight pair with a distant third star. A planet there would have one stable era that never ends. Load Real α Centauri to compare. The full analysis is in the project documentation.
